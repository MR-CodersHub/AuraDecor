The Potter's Wheel:
Priya Sharma's Journey
Through Three Generations

By Priya Sharma · November 2026 · 8 min read · Jaipur, Rajasthan
"Every time I sit at my wheel, I feel like the clay is telling me what it wants to become. My hands just help it get there."
— Priya Sharma, master potter, Sanganer, Jaipur

In a sunlit workshop tucked into the narrow lanes of Sanganer — the ancient potter's quarter on the outskirts of Jaipur — Priya Sharma begins her day before the city wakes. By the time the first muezzin's call drifts over the rooftops, her wheel is already spinning. The clay has warmed in her hands. The day's first vessel is beginning to take shape.

Priya is a third-generation potter in a village where pottery is not simply a craft — it is a language spoken across centuries, passed from grandmother to mother to daughter in the gesture of wet hands on a turning wheel. Her grandmother, Kamla Devi, learned from her mother. Her mother, Shanti, taught Priya. And now Priya teaches eight women in her collective, some of whom had never touched clay before they found their way to her workshop.

The Architecture of Patience

"My grandmother said the clay teaches patience," Priya tells us, her fingers still bearing the warm-red stain of the morning's first clay. "You can't rush a pot, any more than you can rush a child. It tells you when it's ready." She pauses, pressing a thumb into the wall of a vessel to check its thickness. It is a gesture so practiced it has become involuntary — she doesn't even look at her hands when she does it.

The wheel Priya uses today is the same kick-wheel her grandmother worked at for forty years. It is worn smooth in the places where hands have rested, the timber stained the colour of wet earth. Around it, the shelves of her workshop are lined with pots in various stages of becoming — raw, leather-hard, bisque-fired, glazed. The whole arc of a pot's life is visible in a single glance across the room.

From Sanganer to the World

Priya's journey into contemporary pottery design began when she received a scholarship to the Rajasthan School of Arts at eighteen. It was her first encounter with art history — with the idea that the vessels her grandmother had made for kitchens and temples had cousins in museums around the world. "I saw a Korean celadon bowl in a book and I thought: this is what clay can aspire to. And then I thought: but Dadi's pots are just as beautiful."

She returned to Sanganer with new knowledge and an old reverence. She began experimenting with glazes — mixing traditional mineral pigments with her own colour intuitions, achieving effects that felt both ancient and completely contemporary. Her signature glaze — the warm amber-to-terracotta gradient she calls "sunrise" — took three years to perfect. It now defines the AuraDecor Pottery Collection.

"The kiln is the last act of faith. You close the door and you have to trust. That is the most spiritual part of this work."
— Priya Sharma

The Artisan Journey: Step by Step

1

Sourcing the Clay

Priya works exclusively with red terracotta clay from nearby Sanganer, where the mineral-rich soil produces clay with a distinctive warmth. She tests each new batch for plasticity and moisture content before accepting it for use in her workshop.

2

Wedging & Centering

Raw clay is wedged thoroughly by hand to remove air pockets that could cause cracks during firing. It is then centred on the kick-wheel — a process requiring both physical force and extraordinary sensitivity. Even after 25 years, Priya says centering never becomes automatic.

3

Throwing & Shaping

The centered clay is opened and the walls are drawn upward, shaped by the pressure of both hands working in concert. For each piece in the Sunrise series, Priya applies additional pressure at the shoulder to create the distinctive widening form before narrowing the neck.

4

Drying & Trimming

Freshly thrown pots are covered loosely and left to reach leather-hard stage over 24-48 hours, depending on the weather. Priya then trims the base and foot ring on the wheel, refining the form and ensuring even wall thickness throughout.

5

Bisque Firing

Bone-dry pieces are loaded into the wood-fired kiln and taken to 900°C. This bisque firing removes the last moisture and chemical water from the clay, creating a porous ceramic that will accept glaze. The firing takes 8-10 hours; cooling takes another 24.

6

Glazing & Glaze Firing

Priya applies three layers of her sunrise glaze by hand, using a combination of dipping and brushwork to achieve the characteristic gradient. A second firing at 1120°C melts the glaze into a permanent, food-safe surface. No two pieces emerge from the kiln with identical glaze patterns.

The Collective: Passing It Forward

Today, Priya's workshop has expanded into the adjacent building that once housed a small printing press. Eight women work alongside her — some wheel-throwing, some hand-building, some glazing. Three of them have now been working with Priya for more than five years and have begun developing their own distinctive forms.

"I didn't set out to create a collective," Priya admits. "It happened because neighbours kept asking if they could come and learn. And I realised — if I don't teach, this knowledge doesn't just leave my family. It leaves Sanganer. It leaves India." She shakes her head slightly, the weight of that responsibility visible. "We can't let it leave."
